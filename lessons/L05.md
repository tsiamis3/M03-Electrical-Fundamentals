# Lesson 5 — Primary cells and lead-acid batteries

A **cell** is one electrochemical unit; a **battery** is several cells connected together. Today: cells you throw away (primary) and the classic rechargeable aircraft battery, the lead-acid (secondary). How it is built, what happens chemically, how it is rated, tested and charged.

## Primary and secondary cells

| | Primary cell | Secondary cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rechargeable? | **no**: the reaction consumes an electrode | **yes**: the reaction is reversible |
| Recharging attempt | dangerous, can explode | normal maintenance |
| Examples | carbon-zinc, alkaline, silver-oxide, lithium (non-rechargeable) | lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, NiMH, lithium-ion |
| On the aircraft | flashlights, some emergency equipment | main and emergency batteries |

## The dry cell (carbon-zinc)

- **Positive** electrode (cathode, +): a **carbon (graphite) rod** in the centre.
- **Electrolyte**: a moist **paste**, not a liquid ("dry" cell).
- **Negative** electrode (anode, −): the **zinc case** itself.
- As it discharges, the zinc is slowly **consumed**. About **1.5 V** per cell.

**Alkaline** cell: zinc and manganese dioxide (Zn/MnO₂) with an alkaline electrolyte. Same 1.5 V, but **higher energy density** and **longer shelf life** than carbon-zinc.

## Lead-acid battery: construction

| Part | Material / job |
| --- | --- |
| Positive plates | **lead dioxide (PbO₂)**, brown |
| Negative plates | **spongy lead (Pb)**, grey |
| Electrolyte | **sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) + distilled water** |
| Separators | porous, ribbed on the positive side: keep plates apart, let electrolyte circulate, let sediment fall to the bottom |
| Case | hard rubber or plastic, acid-resistant |
| Vent cap | non-spill: lets gas out, a lead weight closes the hole in inverted flight |
| Cell straps | connect the cells **in series** |

- Each cell has many plates; all positives are joined together and all negatives are joined together. More plate area means more current and more capacity.
- Every positive plate sits **between two negatives**, so there is **one more negative plate** than positive.

### The battery box and venting

- The **battery box** is acid-resistant metal: mechanical protection and electrical shielding.
- **Two vent tubes**: one takes in air from the slipstream; the other leads to the **sump jar**.
- The sump jar holds a felt pad soaked in **sodium bicarbonate (baking soda)** solution, which **neutralizes** the acid fumes before they go overboard.
- **Quick-disconnect** plug: covers the terminals (no accidental shorts) and fits only one way (no reversed polarity).

## Lead-acid: chemistry

**Discharging** (electrons leave the negative plates, go through the load, and arrive at the positive plates):

- Both plates turn into **lead sulphate (PbSO₄)**.
- The acid is used up and **water** is formed.
- So the electrolyte gets **weaker** and its **specific gravity falls**.

**Charging** reverses everything: lead sulphate turns back into Pb and PbO₂, and acid returns to the electrolyte.

<div class="formula">Discharge:  Pb + PbO₂ + 2 H₂SO₄  →  2 PbSO₄ + 2 H₂O      (charge: ←)</div>

| State | Specific gravity (SG) |
| --- | --- |
| Fully charged | ≈ 1.270–1.300 |
| Discharged | ≈ 1.150 |

## Ratings

**Voltage** depends only on the **number of cells in series**: about **2 V per cell** (2.1–2.2 V on open circuit when fully charged).

| Battery | Cells in series |
| --- | --- |
| 12 V | 6 |
| 24 V | 12 |

**Capacity** in **ampere-hours (Ah)**: current × time it can supply.

<div class="formula">Capacity (Ah) = I (A) × t (h)</div>

- 1 Ah = 1 A for 1 h = 2 A for ½ h = ⅓ A for 3 h.
- For aircraft batteries the rating is based on a **5-hour discharge**. At higher currents the battery heats up and delivers **fewer** Ah.
- Capacity depends on the **total effective plate area**.

| Connecting batteries | Voltage | Capacity (Ah) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In **series** | adds up | stays the same |
| In **parallel** (same voltage) | stays the same | adds up |

## Life cycle

- **Cycle life**: the number of full charge/discharge cycles until capacity falls below **80%** of rated. Typically **500–1 300 cycles**.
- What shortens it:
  - **Over-discharging** → excess sulphation.
  - **Too fast charging or discharging** → overheating, active material **shed** from the plates. The shed material collects at the bottom and can short the plates.
  - Leaving a battery **discharged for a long time** → permanent damage.

## Testing with a hydrometer

The state of charge is read from the **specific gravity** of the electrolyte (its density compared with water), using a **hydrometer**.

- A weighted glass float in a syringe. Denser electrolyte → the float rides **higher**. So the **highest reading (1.300) is at the bottom** of the scale.
- Fully charged: about **30% acid, 70% water** by volume, SG ≈ 1.300.

| SG reading | State of charge |
| --- | --- |
| 1.300 – 1.275 | high |
| 1.275 – 1.240 | medium |
| 1.240 – 1.200 | **low: recharge now** |

Aircraft batteries are small and loads are heavy, so "low" may still mean ~50% charge, but it is treated as needing immediate recharge.

**Rules:**

- **Temperature correction**: none needed between **21 °C and 32 °C**. Outside that range, add or subtract the correction from the hydrometer's scale or the manufacturer's chart.
- Take readings **before** adding distilled water, never after.
- Only **distilled water** is ever added (to replace evaporation).
- **Acid on skin**: wash with plenty of water, then apply **bicarbonate of soda**.

## Internal resistance

A real battery behaves like an ideal emf **E** with a small **internal resistance r** in series. Under load, part of the voltage is lost inside:

<div class="formula">V_terminal = E − I × r      r = (V_no-load − V_load) / I</div>

- r **increases with age**, as lead sulphate builds up on the plates.
- A battery with high r gets hot under load and its voltage sags.

## Charging

Charge by passing DC **in the opposite direction** to discharge. The charger must overcome the emf **and** the internal voltage drop:

<div class="formula">V_charge = E + I × r</div>

Example: 12-cell battery, open-circuit 12 × 2.2 = **26.4 V**; it needs about **28 V** to charge.

| Method | Current | Pros / cons | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Constant voltage** | starts high, **tapers off** to ~1 A at full charge | faster, less supervision | **on the aircraft**: generator held constant by the voltage regulator |
| **Constant current** | almost constant throughout | slower; risk of **overcharging** near the end | shop chargers |

### Charging safety

Charging releases **hydrogen and oxygen**: an explosive mixture.

- **Loosen the vent caps** but leave them in place.
- **No flames, sparks or other ignition sources** nearby.
- Switch **off** the charger (remotely) **before** connecting or disconnecting the battery.

> Remember: PbO₂ positive, Pb negative, H₂SO₄ electrolyte. Discharge → both plates to PbSO₄, SG falls.
> 2 V per cell, 6 cells = 12 V, 12 cells = 24 V. Series adds volts, parallel adds Ah.
