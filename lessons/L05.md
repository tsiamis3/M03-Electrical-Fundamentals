# Lesson 5 — Primary cells and lead-acid batteries

<p class="ref">Course notes: <b>3.05 DC Sources of Electricity</b>, slides 1–23</p>

The aircraft battery starts the engine or APU, powers the essential bus when the generators fail, and steadies the DC system. Today: cells that cannot be recharged, and the lead-acid battery, from its construction to testing and charging.

## Primary and secondary cells

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 3</p>

| | Primary cell | Secondary cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recharge | **no**: one electrode is used up | **yes**: the reaction reverses |
| Examples | carbon-zinc, alkaline, silver-oxide, lithium | lead-acid, NiCad, NiMH, Li-ion |

**The dry cell (carbon-zinc):**

- A **carbon (graphite) rod** in the middle is the **positive (+)** terminal.
- It sits in an electrolyte **paste**, inside a **zinc can**, which is the **negative (−)** terminal.
- On discharge the zinc is consumed, so the reaction cannot be reversed. Trying to recharge a primary cell is dangerous: it can explode.

## Alkaline cells

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 4</p>

- Reaction between **zinc** and **manganese dioxide** (Zn/MnO₂) in an alkaline electrolyte.
- Same voltage as carbon-zinc (1.5 V), but **higher energy density** and **longer shelf life**.
- Special rechargeable alkaline cells also exist.

## Lead-acid cell: construction

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 5–6</p>

| Part | Material / job |
| --- | --- |
| Positive plates | **lead dioxide (PbO₂)** |
| Negative plates | **spongy lead (Pb)** |
| Electrolyte | **sulphuric acid + distilled water** |
| Separators | porous; stop the plates touching (short circuit); ribs let electrolyte circulate and sediment fall |
| Case | hard rubber (or plastic) per cell, with a non-spill vent cap |

- Many plates per cell give more current. All positives are joined together, and all negatives together.
- Each positive plate sits between two negatives, so there is always **one more negative plate** than positive.
- The vent cap hole lets you test the electrolyte and add water.

## Vent plug, battery box, sump, quick disconnect

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 7–9</p>

- **Vent plug**: a lead weight lets gas out through a small hole in level flight and **closes the hole in inverted flight**, so acid does not spill.
- **Cell straps** join the cells **in series**. The cells sit in an **acid-resistant battery box** (shielding and protection) with a removable top.
- **Venting**: air from the slipstream enters through one tube, sweeps the battery gases out through the other, into the **sump jar**. The sump has a felt pad soaked in **sodium bicarbonate (baking soda)**, which neutralizes the acid fumes before they go overboard.
- **Quick disconnect**: a receptacle covers the terminal posts (no accidental shorts). The plug is turned in by a hand wheel and fits **only one way**, so the leads cannot be reversed.

## Lead-acid: chemistry

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 10</p>

- Electrolyte: sulphuric acid and distilled water, **specific gravity (SG) about 1.270–1.300** when fully charged. It contains H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions.
- **On discharge:**
  - electrons leave the negative plate and reach the positive plate through the external circuit;
  - the sulphate joins the lead of **both** plates and forms **lead sulphate (PbSO₄)**;
  - oxygen from the PbO₂ joins hydrogen and forms **water**.
- So on discharge the acid goes into the plates and water is made. The electrolyte gets **weaker**, and its SG **falls** to about **1.150**.
- Charging reverses all of this.

## Ratings

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 11–12</p>

<div class="formula">One lead-acid cell ≈ 2 V (nominal)
12 V battery = 6 cells in series      24 V battery = 12 cells in series
Capacity (Ah) = current (A) × time (h)</div>

- A 1 Ah battery gives 1 A for 1 h, or 2 A for ½ h, or ⅓ A for 3 h. In reality, the **faster** you discharge it, the **fewer** Ah you get: heavy current heats the battery and lowers its efficiency.
- Aircraft batteries are rated at the **5-hour** discharge rate. A 40 Ah battery is rated for 8 A for 5 h.
- Capacity depends on the **total plate area**:

| Connection | Voltage | Capacity (Ah) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Series | adds up | stays the same |
| Parallel | stays the same | adds up |

## Life cycle

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 13</p>

- **Life cycle**: the number of full charge/discharge cycles until capacity falls below **80 %** of rated. Typically 500–1 300 cycles.
- What shortens it:
  - **over-discharge** → excess sulphation;
  - **too fast charge or discharge** → overheating, **shedding** of active material → sediment shorts the plates;
  - being left **discharged for a long time** → permanent damage.

## Testing with a hydrometer

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 14–18</p>

The state of charge shows in the **density** of the electrolyte, measured as **specific gravity** with a **hydrometer**: a weighted glass float in a syringe.

- Denser electrolyte → the float rides **higher**. So the **largest** number (1.300) is at the **bottom** of the scale.
- A fully charged aircraft battery: about 30 % acid, 70 % water by volume, SG ≈ 1.300.

| SG reading | State of charge |
| --- | --- |
| 1.300 – 1.275 | high |
| 1.275 – 1.240 | medium |
| 1.240 – 1.200 | low → **recharge now** |

- "Low" may still be about 50 % charge. It counts as low because aircraft batteries are small and face heavy loads.
- **Temperature**: no correction between **21 °C and 32 °C**. Outside that range, **add or subtract** the correction from the hydrometer's own scale or the manufacturer's chart.
- Read **before** adding distilled water, never just after: the water needs time to mix.
- **Safety**: sulphuric acid burns skin and clothes. Wash with plenty of water, then apply **bicarbonate of soda**.

## Internal resistance and charging voltage

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 19–20</p>

To charge, push DC through the battery **opposite** to the discharge current. The charger must beat the battery's own voltage **plus** the drop across its internal resistance:

<div class="formula">Charging voltage = open-circuit voltage + I × r
Internal resistance:  r = (no-load voltage − load voltage) / load current</div>

- Example: 12 cells × 2.2 V = 26.4 V open circuit. It takes about **28 V** to charge it.
- Internal resistance **grows with age**, as lead sulphate builds up. Circuit diagrams pretend it is zero; the technician must not.

## Charging methods and safety

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 21–23</p>

| | Constant voltage | Constant current |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current | high at first, **tapers** to about 1 A when full | stays almost constant |
| Time and supervision | less | more; risk of **overcharging** near the end |
| Where | **on the aircraft** (generator + voltage regulator) | in the battery shop |

**Safety while charging:**

- Charging releases **hydrogen and oxygen**, an explosive mixture.
- Loosen the vent caps but leave them in place.
- No flames or sparks nearby.
- Switch the charger off (remote switch) **before** connecting or disconnecting the battery.

> 2 V per cell: 12 V = 6 cells, 24 V = 12 cells.
> Series adds volts, parallel adds Ah.
> SG falls on discharge; 1.300 at the bottom of the float.
> Aircraft charges at constant voltage.

## Corrections to the course notes

- **Slides 10 and 16** give two values for a full charge, SG **1.270** and **1.300**. Both occur: it depends on the battery. Use the manufacturer's value. For the exam, the bands above are those of slide 16.
- **Slides 11 and 19**: "2 V per cell" is the **nominal** voltage; **2.2 V** is the **open-circuit** voltage of a fully charged cell. They do not contradict each other.
- **Slide 3**: a dry cell is a **galvanic** cell (it makes electricity from chemistry), not an "electrolytic" cell (which uses electricity to drive a reaction).
