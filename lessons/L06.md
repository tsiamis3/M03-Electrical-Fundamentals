# Lesson 6 — NiCad, SLA, Li-ion, thermocouples, photocells

<p class="ref">Course notes: <b>3.05 DC Sources of Electricity</b>, slides 24–41</p>

The rest of the DC sources: the nickel-cadmium battery of many turbine aircraft, sealed lead-acid and lithium-ion batteries, connecting cells in series and parallel, internal resistance, and two small sources used as sensors: thermocouples and photocells.

## Nickel-cadmium: chemistry and construction

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 24–25</p>

| Part | Material |
| --- | --- |
| Positive plate (charged) | **nickel oxyhydroxide (NiOOH)** |
| Negative plate (charged) | **sponge cadmium (Cd)** |
| Electrolyte | **potassium hydroxide (KOH)**, 20–34 % by weight in distilled water: an **alkali** |

- **Sintered plates**: thin, very porous nickel grids filled with active material. Ideal for **very high charge and discharge rates** (engine starting).
- **Pocket plates**: active material pressed into perforated steel pockets. Little shedding.
- Open-circuit voltage: about **1.25 V per cell**.

## Nickel-cadmium: operation

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 26–27</p>

- **Charging**: the negative plates lose oxygen and become metallic cadmium; the positive plates become more oxidized.
- Near the end of the charge, and on overcharge, the cells **gas**: water splits into hydrogen (at −) and oxygen (at +). A full charge always needs a little gassing, so some water is used.
- **Discharge** reverses the reaction. The plates **absorb** electrolyte on discharge and release it on charge.
- So the electrolyte level is **highest at full charge**. **Add water only when the battery is fully charged.**

## Changing from lead-acid to NiCad

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 27</p>

Acid and alkali neutralize each other and destroy both batteries, so the two types are **never** mixed. When a NiCad replaces a lead-acid battery:

1. Clean and dry the compartment, removing all traces of acid.
2. Wash and neutralize it with **ammonia** or **boric acid** solution, and let it dry.
3. Paint it with **alkali-resistant varnish**.
4. Soak the sump pad in **3 % boric acid** (not bicarbonate of soda) before connecting the vents.

## NiCad maintenance and safety

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 28–29</p>

- Add distilled water to replace what overcharging used.
- Keep inter-cell connectors **torqued** correctly.
- Keep cell tops clean and dry: spilled electrolyte makes **ground paths**. Clean with distilled water.
- KOH is **caustic**: wear goggles, plastic gloves and an apron. On skin or clothes, neutralize with **vinegar** or **dilute boric acid** (1 lb per gallon of water), then rinse with water.
- Overcharge releases **hydrogen and oxygen** through the relief valves. Ventilate so hydrogen stays **below 1 %** by volume: above **4 %** it can explode.

## Sealed lead-acid (SLA) vs NiCad

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 30–31</p>

| | NiCad | SLA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best at | deep, frequent, high-rate cycling | long **standby** (emergency packs for IRU, standby attitude) |
| Cell matching | up to 20 cells matched by hand, to avoid **cell reversal** | built into the battery |
| Voltage depression ("memory") | yes: from constant overcharge and rare discharge; needs deep-cycle maintenance | no |
| Test equipment | complex | simple; some have **built-in test (BITE)** |

## Nickel-metal hydride (NiMH)

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 32</p>

- Positive electrode like the NiCad (nickel oxyhydroxide). Negative electrode: a **hydrogen-absorbing alloy** instead of cadmium.
- **2–3 times the capacity** of a NiCad of the same size, but much less than lithium-ion.
- Same cell voltage as alkaline cells, with less leakage.

## Lithium-ion

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 33–34</p>

- Negative electrode: **carbon**. Positive electrode: a **metal oxide**. Electrolyte: a **lithium salt in an organic solvent**, which is **flammable**.
- Anode and cathode swap roles between charge and discharge.
- Much lighter and more powerful than NiCad for the same job. The **Boeing 787** uses two **32 V, 8-cell** Li-ion batteries, giving 150 A for power-up.
- Under some conditions they can **overheat and catch fire**, so they need monitoring and protective containment.

## Cells in series and in parallel

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 35–37</p>

| | Series | Parallel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Path | one path | several paths |
| Current | **same** in every part | **adds up** |
| Voltage | **adds up** | **same** across every part |
| One part fails | the whole circuit stops | the others keep working |
| Batteries | volts add, Ah stay the same | Ah add, volts stay the same |

- Four bulbs in series on a 6 V battery: 6 / 4 = **1.5 V** each, maybe too little to glow. In parallel, each bulb gets the full 6 V.
- A lead-acid cell is about 2 V, so a 12 V battery has 6 cells and a 24 V battery has 12, joined in series by heavy straps.

## Internal resistance

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 38</p>

- Internal resistance **grows with age** and use (lead sulphate builds up). Find it from the voltage with and without load: r = (no-load V − load V) / I.
- **Low** internal resistance means **high current on demand**. High internal resistance makes the battery **heat up** and its **voltage drop** under load.
- **Cold raises** it, **heat lowers** it. Starting on a cold morning is harder.
- Lead-acid batteries handle short high-current bursts well but tire on long heavy discharges. Nickel and lithium chemistries respond better.

## Thermocouples

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slides 39–40</p>

- Two different metals joined at **two junctions**:
  - the **hot junction**, where the temperature is measured;
  - the **reference (cold) junction**, at the instrument or data computer.
- The emf depends on the **temperature difference** between them, and is a few **millivolts**.

| Use | Metals |
| --- | --- |
| CHT (piston engines) | iron–constantan, copper–constantan |
| EGT (turbine engines) | **chromel–alumel** |

- **Fire detection**: normally both junctions are at about the same temperature, so there is no output. A fire heats one junction: the emf is amplified and sets off the alarm.
- Limitation: **accuracy**. Errors below 1 °C are hard to achieve.

## Photocells

<p class="ref">Notes 3.05, slide 41</p>

- Light is made of **photons**. The energy of a photon depends on its **frequency** (colour): blue light carries more energy per photon than red.
- A photon that hits a semiconductor can give an electron enough energy to break free and flow as **current**.
- The current can drive a relay or a control circuit: light sensors, automatic switching.

> NiCad: KOH (alkali), 1.25 V, add water only when fully charged.
> Never mix acid and alkali: separate tools, sumps, rooms.
> Series adds V, parallel adds A and Ah.
> Thermocouple: millivolts from a temperature difference.

## Corrections to the course notes

- **Slide 24** labels the positive plate "anode" and the negative "cathode". On **discharge** it is the other way round: the negative plate is the anode (where electrons leave). Slide 33 says it correctly: the roles swap between charge and discharge. Safer to say **positive** and **negative** plate.
- **Slide 39**: the thermocouple emf is proportional to the temperature **difference** between the junctions, not to the temperature, as slide 40 itself says for fire detection.
- **Slide 33**: Li-ion cells are not generally "kept pressurized". The danger is the flammable electrolyte and **thermal runaway**.
- **Slide 37** says the battery box is plastic, while slide 8 says acid-resistant metal. Both exist.
