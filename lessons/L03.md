# Lesson 3 — Electrical terminology and SI prefixes

<p class="ref">Course notes: <b>3.03 Electrical Terminology</b>, slides 1–21</p>

Today we learn the vocabulary of the whole module: voltage, current, resistance, conductance, charge, and the two directions people draw current in. Each one comes with its symbol, its unit and the prefixes we write it with.

## SI prefixes

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slides 3–4</p>

Electrical values range from millionths to millions, so we use prefixes:

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| giga | G | 10⁹ = 1 000 000 000 | 1 GΩ insulation resistance |
| mega | M | 10⁶ = 1 000 000 | 1 MΩ |
| kilo | k | 10³ = 1 000 | 1 kV |
| — | — | 1 | 1 V |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ = 1/1 000 | 500 mA |
| micro | μ | 10⁻⁶ = 1/1 000 000 | 10 μF |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ | 100 nF |
| pico | p | 10⁻¹² | 47 pF |

- Big unit → small unit: **multiply**. 2.5 kV = 2.5 × 1 000 = 2 500 V.
- Small unit → big unit: **divide**. 250 mA = 250 / 1 000 = 0.25 A.
- Each step in the table is × 1 000. **Capital M** is mega, **small m** is milli.

## Potential difference

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slide 5</p>

- **Potential difference (PD)** between two points is the **work done** to move a unit of positive charge from one point to the other.
- Think of it as a difference of **electrical pressure**. Current flows only between points at **different** potentials.
- A wire has very little opposition to current, so the PD along it is tiny and normally taken as **zero**. A PD appears across anything that opposes the current (a lamp, a resistor).
- Unit: the **volt (V)**.

## Electromotive force

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slide 6</p>

- The **electromotive force (emf, symbol ε or E)** is the push a **source** (battery, generator, ground power unit) provides to move electrons around a circuit.
- Typical aircraft batteries give **12 V or 24 V** between their terminals.
- Outside the source, electrons leave the **negative** terminal and are attracted to the **positive** terminal.

| | emf | Potential difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | across a **source** | between any two points, e.g. across a load |
| What it does | supplies energy to the charges | energy taken from the charges |
| Unit | volt | volt |

## Voltage, energy and charge

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slide 7</p>

<div class="formula">V = W / Q        volts = joules / coulombs        1 V = 1 J/C</div>

V is the potential difference, W the energy given or absorbed (J), Q the charge moved (C).
Example: 120 J of energy moves 5 C of charge → V = 120 / 5 = 24 V.

## The water-tank picture

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slides 8–9</p>

Tank A at 10 psi, tank B at 2 psi, joined by a pipe with a valve. Open the valve: water flows from A to B until the levels are equal.

- What drives the flow is the **difference**, 10 − 2 = **8 psi**, not the 10 psi in tank A.
- In the same way, current is driven by the **difference** in potential between two points. Electrons move from the point with an **excess** of electrons (−) to the point with a **deficiency** (+), as long as there is a path.

## The volt defined through power

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slide 10</p>

**Power (P)** is the rate of doing work, in **watts (W)**. The volt is defined as:

> 1 volt is the PD between two points of a conductor carrying 1 ampere, when 1 watt is dissipated between them.

<div class="formula">V = P / I        1 V = 1 W / 1 A</div>

Example: a 28 V lamp draws 2 A → it dissipates P = 28 × 2 = 56 W.

## Current

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slides 11–12</p>

- **Current (I)** is the **rate** at which charge flows. In metals the moving charges are the **free electrons**. They leave the **negative** terminal of the source and are attracted to the **positive** one.

<div class="formula">I = Q / t        amperes = coulombs / seconds
1 A = 1 C/s = 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons per second</div>

Example: 30 C pass a point in 1 minute → I = 30 / 60 = 0.5 A.

## Direct current and drift velocity

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slides 13–14</p>

- Current that flows in **one direction** is **direct current (DC)**. Current that reverses periodically is **AC** (unit E).
- Free electrons move randomly, like gas molecules. A voltage adds a slow **average** motion towards the positive end, interrupted by constant collisions with the atoms of the metal: the **drift velocity**. It is **very slow**, a fraction of a millimetre per second.
- Yet a lamp lights **at once**. Think of a tube full of steel balls: push one in at one end and one comes out at the other end immediately. The **push** travels almost instantly, even though each ball (electron) moves slowly.

## Resistance

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slide 15</p>

- **Resistance (R)** is the opposition to current. Unit: the **ohm (Ω)**.
- For a given voltage: **more resistance → less current**, less resistance → more current. For most conductors this is a straight-line relation: **Ohm's law** (lesson 7).

## Conductance

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slide 16</p>

**Conductance (G)** is the **ease** with which current flows: the opposite of resistance. Unit: the **siemens (S)**, 1 S = 1 A/V.

<div class="formula">G = I / E        G = 1 / R</div>

- At constant voltage the current is **proportional** to G: double G → double I; G down to ¼ → I down to ¼.
- Example: 24 V drives 0.5 A through a component → G = 0.5 / 24 = 0.0208 S = 20.8 mS (R = 48 Ω).

## Charge and Coulomb's law

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slides 17–18</p>

- An atom is charged when its **protons and electrons are not equal**. More electrons → **negative** charge (negative polarity). Fewer electrons → **positive** charge (positive polarity).
- **Charge (Q)** is measured in **coulombs (C)**: 1 C = 6.24 × 10¹⁸ elementary charges. The net charge of an object is the sum over all its atoms.
- Every charged object has an **electric field** around it, proportional to its charge.

**Coulomb's law** (in vacuum, charges q₁ and q₂ in C, distance r in m, force F in N):

<div class="formula">F = k × q₁ × q₂ / r²        k = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²</div>

- Same polarity → repulsive force. Opposite polarity → attractive force.
- Example: 2 μC and 3 μC, 0.1 m apart → F = 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ / 0.1² = **5.4 N**.

## Conventional flow and electron flow

<p class="ref">Notes 3.03, slides 19–21</p>

| | Conventional flow | Electron flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction outside the source | **+ → −** | **− → +** |
| Origin | Benjamin Franklin, before electrons were known | what electrons really do in a metal |
| Used in | most textbooks, circuit symbols (diode arrows) | many maintenance texts |

For troubleshooting it makes no difference which one you use, **as long as you stick to one**.

> V = W/Q, I = Q/t, G = I/E = 1/R.
> 1 V = 1 W / 1 A.
> Electrons drift slowly; the push is instant.
> Conventional + → −, electrons − → +.

## Corrections to the course notes

- **Slide 3**: there are not "12" SI prefixes. Since 2022 there are **24** (from quecto, 10⁻³⁰, to quetta, 10³⁰). The symbol of micro is **μ**, not "q".
- **Slide 7**: ε is used both for the emf (slide 6) and for the energy. Here we write **V = W/Q**, with W for energy.
- **Slide 12**: 6.24 × 10¹⁸ is not "6.24 billion". It is 6 240 000 000 000 000 000 (6.24 quintillion).
- **Slide 13**: collisions do not "knock other free electrons from their atoms". The electrons **bounce off** the vibrating atoms of the metal, and that is what keeps the drift slow.
- **Slide 17**: an atom with a **negative** charge has **negative** polarity, not positive.
- **Slide 17**: everyday objects do **not** hold "many coulombs". Static charges are nanocoulombs to microcoulombs. Two charges of 1 C, 1 m apart, would push with 9 × 10⁹ N.
