# Lesson 3 — Electrical terminology and SI prefixes

Today we name the quantities we will use for the rest of the course: charge, current, voltage (potential difference and emf), resistance and conductance. We also learn to move between µ, m, k and M without losing a factor of 1000.

## SI prefixes

Electrical values run from millionths of a unit to millions. A prefix in front of the unit replaces the power of ten.

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| giga | G | 10⁹ = 1 000 000 000 | 1 GΩ insulation test |
| mega | M | 10⁶ = 1 000 000 | 2.2 MΩ resistor |
| kilo | k | 10³ = 1 000 | 4.7 kΩ resistor |
| — | — | 10⁰ = 1 | 28 V, 5 A |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ = 1/1 000 | 40 mV from a thermocouple |
| micro | µ | 10⁻⁶ = 1/1 000 000 | 10 µF capacitor |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ | 470 nF |
| pico | p | 10⁻¹² | 100 pF |

<div class="formula">1 kV = 1 000 V      1 V = 0.001 kV      1 mA = 0.001 A      1 µA = 0.001 mA</div>

How to convert:

- **To a bigger prefix** (mA → A, V → kV): the number gets **smaller**. Divide by 1 000 per step.
- **To a smaller prefix** (A → mA, MΩ → kΩ): the number gets **bigger**. Multiply by 1 000 per step.

> Capital M = mega (10⁶). Small m = milli (10⁻³). Mixing them up is a factor of 10⁹.

## Charge (Q)

- An object is **charged** when its numbers of protons and electrons differ. More electrons → **negative**. Fewer electrons → **positive**.
- Unit: the **coulomb (C)**.

<div class="formula">1 C ≈ 6.24 × 10¹⁸ elementary charges      e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C</div>

- Charges create an electric field around them. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract (Lesson 2, Coulomb's law).

## Current (I)

Current is the **rate** at which charge flows past a point.

<div class="formula">I = Q / t      (amperes = coulombs / seconds)</div>

- 1 **ampere (A)** = 1 coulomb passing a point every second.
- Symbol on schematics and in formulas: **I**.
- **DC (direct current)** flows one way. **AC** reverses periodically (unit E of the course).

### Drift velocity

Free electrons already move randomly at high speed. A voltage adds a slow average drift towards the positive end, of the order of a **fraction of a millimetre per second**.

Why does a lamp light at once, then? Picture a tube already full of balls. Push one in at one end and one comes out of the other end immediately. The wire is already full of free electrons; the **push** travels almost at the speed of light, the electrons themselves do not.

## Potential difference and electromotive force (V, E)

**Potential difference** is the energy given to (or taken from) each coulomb of charge that moves between two points.

<div class="formula">V = W / Q      (volts = joules / coulombs)</div>

- 1 **volt** = 1 joule per coulomb.
- Equivalent definition from the deck: 1 V is the potential difference between two points of a conductor carrying **1 A** when **1 W** is dissipated between them (V = P / I).
- Voltage is always **between two points**. "The voltage at a point" means "between that point and ground (or a reference)".

**Electromotive force (emf, E)** is the "push" a **source** (battery, generator, ground power unit) gives. Without an emf there is no steady current.

| | emf | Potential difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | inside a source | across any two points, usually a load |
| What it does | gives energy to the charges | charges give energy up (heat, light, motion) |
| Unit | volt | volt |
| Example | 24 V battery with nothing connected | voltage across a lamp |

### The water-tank picture

Two tanks at 10 psi and 2 psi, joined by a pipe. Water flows because of the **8 psi difference**, not because of the 10 psi. In the same way, current flows because of a **difference** of potential. Two points at the same potential (even 10 000 V each) push no current between them. That is why a bird on a power line is safe.

Wires have very little opposition, so almost no voltage is "used" along them. Where opposition is large (a lamp, a resistor), the voltage drop appears across that component.

## Resistance (R)

Resistance is the **opposition** to current.

- Unit: the **ohm (Ω)**.
- For a given voltage: **more resistance → less current**, less resistance → more current.
- This is **Ohm's law** (Lesson 7):

<div class="formula">I = V / R</div>

## Conductance (G)

Conductance is the **ease** with which current flows: the inverse of resistance.

<div class="formula">G = 1 / R = I / V      unit: siemens (S) = A/V</div>

- 1 S: a component that passes 1 A with 1 V across it.
- For a fixed voltage, current is **proportional** to conductance: double G → double I; quarter G → quarter I.
- Old unit you may still see: the **mho** (ohm backwards), symbol ℧. 1 mho = 1 S.

## Conventional flow and electron flow

| | Conventional current | Electron flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction outside the source | **+ → −** | **− → +** |
| Origin | Benjamin Franklin, before electrons were known | what electrons actually do |
| Where used | most schematics, arrows on diode and transistor symbols | many maintenance texts, including parts of this course |

Both give the same answers if you use **one of them consistently**. Diode and transistor symbols point in the conventional direction.

## Summary of quantities

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Unit symbol | Defining relation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Charge | Q | coulomb | C | 1 C = 6.24 × 10¹⁸ e |
| Current | I | ampere | A | I = Q / t |
| Potential difference / emf | V, E | volt | V | V = W / Q |
| Resistance | R | ohm | Ω | R = V / I |
| Conductance | G | siemens | S | G = 1 / R |
| Energy | W | joule | J | W = V × Q |
| Power | P | watt | W | P = V × I |

> Watch the symbols: **W** is the symbol for energy (work) and also the unit symbol of power (watt).

## Notes on the course deck

- Slide 3 writes the micro symbol as "q". The symbol is **µ** (Greek mu).
- Slide 7 uses ε for energy and E for voltage in V = W/Q. In this course: **W** (or energy in joules) / **Q**. Keep **E** for emf.
- Slide 12: "one coulomb equals 6.24 billion (10¹⁸) electrons". It is **6.24 × 10¹⁸**, a billion billion, not a billion.
- Slide 13: collisions do not "knock other free electrons" along. Electrons scatter off the vibrating lattice; that is what limits the drift and causes resistance.
- Slide 17: "an atom with negative charge is said to have positive electric polarity". Wrong: a negative charge has **negative** polarity.
- "Siemen" → the unit is **siemens**, singular and plural.
