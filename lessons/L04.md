# Lesson 4 — Generation of electricity + quiz A

Every source of electricity turns some other form of energy into electrical energy. The syllabus names seven: **light, heat, friction, pressure, chemical action, magnetism and motion**. Today we see what each one is and where it shows up on an aircraft. In the second hour: **quiz A** on lessons 1–4.

## The seven sources at a glance

| Source | Energy in | Device / effect | Typical output | On the aircraft |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light | light (photons) | photovoltaic (solar) cell, photocell | ~0.5 V per silicon cell | light sensors, satellites |
| Heat | heat | thermocouple (Seebeck effect) | millivolts | EGT, CHT, ITT, fire detection |
| Friction | mechanical (rubbing) | static electricity (triboelectric) | high voltage, almost no current | a hazard: ESD, refuelling, static wicks |
| Pressure | mechanical stress | piezoelectric crystal | small voltage pulses | vibration sensors, pickups, microphones |
| Chemical action | chemical | battery (cell) | 1.2–2 V per cell | main and emergency batteries |
| Magnetism and motion | mechanical (rotation) | generator (induction) | 28 V DC, 115 V AC | engine-driven generators, alternators |

Only **chemical action** and **magnetism and motion** give the large, steady power an aircraft needs. The others are used mostly for **sensing**.

## Light — photovoltaic cells

- Light arrives as **photons**. A photon absorbed in a **semiconductor** (silicon) can free an electron from its atom.
- Each freed electron leaves behind a **hole** (a missing electron), which behaves like a positive charge.
- Inside the cell, electrons and holes are pushed in **opposite directions**, so a voltage appears across the cell and a current can flow in an external circuit.
- Uses: satellites and space probes, calculators, watches, light sensors.

## Heat — thermocouples (Seebeck effect)

A **thermocouple** is two **dissimilar metals** joined at a junction.

- Heat the **hot (measuring) junction** while the **cold (reference) junction** stays at a known temperature.
- A small emf appears, roughly **proportional to the temperature difference** between the two junctions.
- If both junctions are at the **same temperature**, there is **no emf**, whatever that temperature is.
- Different metal pairs give different voltages.

<div class="formula">emf ∝ (T_hot − T_cold)      (millivolts)</div>

| Pair | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| Chromel–alumel (type K) | turbine EGT / ITT |
| Iron–constantan (type J), copper–constantan (type T) | piston engine CHT |

Advantages: cheap, robust, interchangeable, wide temperature range, need **no power supply**. Covered in more detail in Lesson 6.

## Friction — static electricity

Rubbing two insulators moves electrons from one to the other (triboelectric effect, Lesson 2). High voltages, tiny charge, no steady current: on an aircraft friction electricity is a **problem to control**, not a source to use.

## Pressure — piezoelectricity

*Piezo* comes from the Greek **πιέζω**, to press.

- Squeeze certain **crystals** (quartz, Rochelle salt, barium titanate and other ceramics): they **polarize** and a voltage appears across them, proportional to the stress.
- **Reverse effect**: apply a voltage and the crystal **changes shape** slightly.

| Direction | Uses |
| --- | --- |
| pressure → voltage | vibration sensors (engine vibration monitoring), microphones, pickups, pressure transducers |
| voltage → movement | earphones and small loudspeakers, buzzers, ultrasonic transducers, quartz oscillators (clocks) |

## Chemical action — cells and batteries

- Two **different electrode materials** in an **electrolyte**.
- A chemical reaction makes one electrode give up electrons more readily than the other. That electrode collects the electrons and becomes the **negative** terminal; the other becomes the **positive** terminal.
- Connect a load: electrons flow through the external circuit from **− to +**, and the reaction continues inside the cell.
- Example (alkaline cell): **zinc** negative electrode, **manganese dioxide** positive electrode, about 1.5 V.

Primary and secondary cells are the subject of Lessons 5 and 6.

## Magnetism and motion — induction

- Move a **conductor through magnetic flux** (or move the flux past the conductor) and an **emf is induced** in it.
- Close the circuit and a current flows.
- This is how every aircraft **generator and alternator** works. The details come in Lesson 18 (induction) and the machines lessons.

Three things increase the induced emf: a **stronger field**, **faster motion**, and **more conductor** (more turns) cutting the flux.

> Remember the seven: **L**ight, **H**eat, **F**riction, **P**ressure, **C**hemical, **M**agnetism + **M**otion.
> Power sources: chemical and magnetic. Sensors: heat, light, pressure.

## Notes on the course deck

- Slide 4 says the thermocouple voltage is proportional to "the temperature at the junction". It depends on the **difference** between the hot and cold junctions (slide 5 says this correctly).
- Slide 8 says the electrode "that frees more electrons develops a positive charge". It is the other way round: the electrode that gives up electrons (zinc) collects them and is the **negative** terminal.
- Slide 8 calls manganese oxide a metal. It is a metal **oxide** (MnO₂), used as the positive electrode.
