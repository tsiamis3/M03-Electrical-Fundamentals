# Lesson 2 — Static electricity and conduction

<p class="ref">Course notes: <b>3.02 Static Electricity and Conduction</b>, slides 1–17</p>

Yesterday: electricity lives in the outer electrons. Today: what happens when electrons pile up on a surface and stay there (static electricity), why that matters on an aircraft, and how charge moves through solids, liquids, gases and vacuum.

## Static and dynamic electricity

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 3</p>

| | Static | Dynamic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrons | at rest, piled up on a surface | moving: a current |
| Example | a charged balloon, a spark from a door handle | a lamp on a 28 V bus |

- A static charge appears when **two surfaces touch and separate** and at least one of them is an **insulator** (rubber, plastic, glass). Electrons move from one surface to the other, so one ends up **positive** and the other **negative**.
- The charge stays because an insulator gives it no path to leave. When the object comes close to a conductor or ground, the charge is neutralized: that is the **spark** (**static discharge**).
- **Lightning** is the same thing on a huge scale: charge separated inside storm clouds, a discharge that heats the air until it glows (flash) and expands explosively (thunder).

## The triboelectric effect

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 4</p>

When two materials touch, electrons can cross from one to the other:

- materials whose outer electrons are **weakly bound** tend to **lose** electrons → become **positive**;
- materials whose outer shell is **nearly full** tend to **gain** electrons → become **negative**.

This contact charging is the **triboelectric effect**, the main cause of everyday static electricity. Rubbing only increases the number of contact points.

## Attraction and repulsion

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 5</p>

<div class="formula">Like charges repel.      Unlike charges attract.</div>

- Electron–electron: repel. Proton–proton: repel. Electron–proton: attract.
- **Pith balls**: touch both with the same charged glass rod → same charge → they **repel**. Touch one with the glass rod (+) and the other with a plastic rod rubbed with fur (−) → opposite charges → they **attract**.

## Why a charged object attracts a neutral one

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 6</p>

- Bring a charged object near a **neutral** one: the charges inside the neutral object **separate**. The opposite charge moves (or the molecules turn) towards the charged object, the like charge away from it. This is **charge-induced separation** (**polarization**).
- The near side is closer, so the attraction wins over the repulsion: the neutral object is pulled in. Styrofoam on a cat's fur and static cling in clothes work this way.
- The effect is strongest in **conductors**, where electrons are free to move. If part of the conductor is **grounded** during the separation, electrons can leave or enter, and the object keeps a **permanent charge** (charging by induction, below).

## Electrostatic fields and field lines

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slides 7–8</p>

A charged body is surrounded by an **electrostatic field**. We draw it with **lines of force**:

- **Direction**: the way a small **positive** test charge would be pushed. So lines point **away from +** and **towards −**.
- **Strength**: shown by how **close together** the lines are (lines per unit area).
- Lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge, and **never cross**.
- Two like charges: the lines bend away from each other and leave a field-free point between them. Two unlike charges: the lines join them.

## Where charge sits on a conductor

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slides 9–10</p>

Free electrons in a conductor push each other as far apart as they can. The result:

- All excess charge sits on the **outer surface**.
- Inside a **hollow conductor** there is **no charge and no field**. That is why the safest place near a large Van de Graaff generator is *inside* its sphere, and why an aircraft protects its occupants from lightning (a **Faraday cage**).
- On an irregular object the charge is **densest at points and sharp edges** (sharpest curvature). On a flat disk it gathers at the **rim**. This is why aircraft carry **static dischargers (wicks)** with sharp points on the trailing edges: they leak the charge into the air gently, away from the radio antennas.

## Electrostatic discharge (ESD)

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slides 11–12</p>

The static charge on a technician's body can destroy solid-state components and circuit cards, often without a visible spark.

| Causes | Protection |
| --- | --- |
| Touching the connector pins of a card or LRU | Wear a **grounding wrist strap** |
| A carpet-covered toolbox top | Touch the **chassis** of the removed unit first |
| Moving, sliding, unpacking | **Protective caps** on open connectors |
| | Keep ESD-sensitive parts in their **ESD bags** |

Good practice with electronic components (slide 12):

- Ground the test equipment to the circuit **before** you inject or measure a signal.
- Never exceed the maximum test voltage of the components.
- Do not test transistors on ohmmeter ranges that drive more than **1 mA**.
- Solder with a low-wattage iron and a **heat sink**.
- Never pry components off a board.
- **Remove power** before replacing a component.
- Keep the exposed tips of test probes short.

## Static and flammable materials

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 13</p>

Fuel flowing through hoses and filters is a poor conductor, so it **builds up static charge**. A small spark can ignite the fuel vapour. That is why the aircraft and the fuel truck are **bonded and grounded** before refuelling starts.

## Units of charge and Coulomb's law

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 14</p>

- The **elementary charge** *e* is the charge of one proton (+) or one electron (−).
- The unit of charge is the **coulomb (C)**: the charge carried by **1 ampere in 1 second**.

<div class="formula">Q = I × t          1 C = 1 A × 1 s
1 C = 6.24 × 10¹⁸ elementary charges      e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C</div>

**Coulomb's law**: the force between two charges is

- **proportional to the product of the charges**: double one charge → double the force;
- **inversely proportional to the square of the distance**: double the distance → **one quarter** of the force.

(The formula with numbers comes in lesson 3.)

## Charging by contact and by induction

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slides 15–16</p>

**By contact**: a positive rod **touches** a neutral metal bar. Electrons move from the bar into the rod. The bar is left **positive** (the same sign as the rod), and the rod becomes less positive.

**By induction**: the rod **does not touch**.

1. Bring a positive rod near the bar. Electrons gather at the near end; the far end is left positive.
2. Ground the far end (touch it). Electrons flow **in** from ground.
3. Remove the **ground first**, then the rod.
4. The bar is left with an excess of electrons: **negative**, the **opposite** sign of the rod.

> Contact: same sign as the rod. Induction: opposite sign.
> Like repel, unlike attract.
> Charge sits outside, and piles up at points.
> Double the distance → ¼ of the force.

## Conduction in solids, liquids, gases and vacuum

<p class="ref">Notes 3.02, slide 17</p>

| Medium | Who carries the current |
| --- | --- |
| Metals (solid or liquid) | free valence electrons |
| Liquids (electrolytes, e.g. salty water, battery acid) | positive and negative **ions** |
| Gases | normally insulators; in a very strong field they **ionize** (spark, lightning) and ions + electrons carry the current |
| Vacuum | nothing of its own; electrons put into it (from a heated cathode) travel with nothing to stop them |

## Corrections to the course notes

- **Slide 4**: materials that **gain** electrons are those with a **nearly full** outer shell, not a "sparsely filled" one.
- **Slide 6**: the Van de Graaff generator carries charge up on its moving **belt**. Grounding during induction is how you charge a bar (slide 16), not how a Van de Graaff works.
- **Slide 8**: field lines do not "repel each other". The rule is that they **never cross**.
- **Slide 9**: on a metal disk the charge is **not** spread evenly: it gathers at the **rim**, as slide 10 says for sharp edges.
- **Slide 14**: 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons is **one coulomb**. One ampere is one coulomb **per second**.
- **Slide 16**: remove the **ground first**, then the rod. The other way round, the electrons flow back out.
- **Slide 17**:
  - In metals the electrons are shared by the whole crystal (**metallic bonding**), not held in "weak covalent bonds".
  - Water does not "ionize when electricity is applied": the ions (dissolved salts) are already there, and pure water hardly conducts.
  - A vacuum is not an "ideal conductor": it has **no carriers of its own**.
